About

I am a Ph.D. student at the Harris School of Public Policy at the University of Chicago. My research sits at the intersection of labor economics, public economics, and inequality. Current work examines lawful migration pathways and labor markets in the United States, and how trade agreements and labor-law enforcement affect households in developing countries.

Before Harris, I was a research assistant in the World Bank’s Poverty and Equity Global Practice, where I contributed to Peru’s Poverty Assessment, the Country Climate and Development Report, and distributional analysis of fiscal and climate policy. I previously worked as an economist at the Peruvian Institute of Economics (IPE). I hold a B.Sc. and M.Sc. in Economics from Universidad del Pacífico (Lima, Peru).

Research

Published

Does the Enforcement of Labor Regulations Reduce Informality? The Case of Peru

with María Florencia Pinto and Hernán Winkler. Latin American Economic Review 37 (2026). doi

We study the impact of stronger labor inspections in Peru from 2010 to 2019, using the creation of SUNAFIL and its gradual rollout as variation in enforcement. Although decentralization significantly increased fines on formal firms, we find little evidence of meaningful changes in employment or informality.

Working papers

Expanding Lawful Pathways: The Economic Impact of New Migration Policies in the U.S.

Doctoral research

This study investigates how granting deportation protection and work authorization to immigrants in the United States influences economic opportunities for both migrants and native workers. Exploiting the expansion of Temporary Protected Status (TPS), it examines labor-market behavior among beneficiaries and whether the policy creates job competition or complements native labor supply.

Child Mortality and the Unexpected Effect of Deep Trade Agreements

with Alberto Chong and Alejandro Riaño

Using a staggered-adoption difference-in-differences design based on countries’ first signature of a Free Trade Agreement from 1990 to 2019, we find that while earlier unilateral trade liberalization is associated with declining child mortality, broader FTAs are associated with a slower decline. Countries that signed deep FTAs see an increase of 4 deaths per 1,000 live births relative to countries that had not yet signed an FTA in the sample. Effects vary by timing, children’s age, and FTA characteristics.

Presented at City, University of London; University of Surrey; Brunel University; and LACEA-LAMES 2022.
